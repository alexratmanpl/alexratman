A 5-minute scroll about starting a job with AI

Ask, write
or draw?

Before an AI builds anything for you, you can start in three ways. Ask in a chat. Write down what it must do, in a short list called a spec. Or draw what it must look like. The wrong start costs you time, so this page helps you pick.

Step 1 · Three ways to start

Every start has a price

What changes between the starts is how much you decide before the AI builds, and how much it decides for you. "Ask" is not a lazy start. For a small job, it is the right one. The docs for Anthropic's coding tool point the same way: in most cases, a plain first prompt already does the right thing.

Ask. You describe the job in a chat and see what comes back.

Fits when
The job is small and has one clear right result. You can say it in one sentence and check it at a glance.
Goes wrong when
The AI has to guess. It fills every gap with its own choice, and you find out after it has built the thing.

Write. Before anything is built, you and the AI write down what the thing must do, as plain numbered sentences. The list is called a spec.

Fits when
The job has rules that matter, such as sums, saved data or who may see what, and you can check each rule.
Goes wrong when
The job is so small that the writing costs more than the job. Or it is about how things look, and words cannot show that.

Draw. You look at rough drawings of the screens, pick one and change it, before anything is built.

Fits when
People will judge the result by looking: layout, order, mood.
Goes wrong when
There is nothing to look at, or the drawing promises something the content cannot deliver.

Write and draw. Many real jobs have rules and screens. Write down the rules, draw the screens, then keep both.

Fits when
People judge how it looks, and it also handles something that matters, such as money or personal details.
Goes wrong when
The job is small or little is at stake. Then Ask is enough.

Illustrative, not measured. The bars compare the starts with each other, not time or money.

Takeaway

When the choices matter, you want to make them. When they don't, deciding up front only wastes your time.

Step 2 · Five jobs

Pick the start, then see why

Use what you just read. For each job, choose how you would start, and see what would happen. After that, try the other starts to see where they lead.

Try it

Job 1: Tidy a list of dates

You have a list of 40 dates typed in different ways, such as 25 Oct 2026, October 26th 2026 and 27.10.2026. You want each one written as 2026-10-25.

  • You can say it in one sentence.
  • Each date has one clear right answer.
  • A wrong line would show at once.

How do you start? Ask, Write, Draw or Write and draw.

Show the best start

Best start: Ask. You paste the list and type the sentence. You look at the new list. If one line is wrong, you say so and the AI fixes it. This takes a few minutes.

Why: It is small, and you can check the result at a glance. A wrong line shows at once and costs a minute to fix, so there is little to protect.

The other starts:

  • Write. You spend half an hour on numbered rules for a job that takes one sentence to ask. The AI would do the same thing in the end.
  • Draw. There is nothing to look at. The AI would draw boxes around a list of dates.
  • Write and draw. You would write rules and draw screens for a job that has neither.

Job 2: A shopping-list app

You want a small app for your shopping list. You type an item and how many you need, and tick items off as you shop.

  • It has rules: what ticking does, what happens when you add the same item twice, and whether the list is kept.
  • You can check each rule with an example: Milk 2, then Milk 1, should give Milk 3.
  • The screen is only two boxes and a list.

How do you start? Ask, Write, Draw or Write and draw.

Show the best start

Best start: Write. The AI asks what should happen when you add Milk twice. You decide, and each rule gets a check you can run yourself.

Why: It has rules that matter, and you can check each one. The job is small, but size is not the risk. The risk is that the AI would decide the rules for you.

The other starts:

  • Ask. The AI decides what ticking does and what to save. You find out about those choices only if you ask.
  • Draw. You could draw two boxes and a list, but the screen is not the hard part. Nobody has decided what ticking does.
  • Write and draw. You get rules and a drawing. For two boxes and a list, the drawing adds little.

Job 3: A home page for a bakery

A small bakery needs a home page. The owner says: "Friendly and clean, with the loaf of the week on top." She cannot say more than that.

  • "Friendly" and "clean" fit many different pages, so there is no one right page.
  • There are few rules to test. The question is how it looks and what comes first.
  • The owner will judge the result by looking at it.

How do you start? Ask, Write, Draw or Write and draw.

Show the best start

Best start: Draw. You get three rough drawings that differ in what comes first. The owner points at one, and you start from there.

Why: People will judge it by looking, and words fit many different pages. A drawing lets the owner choose instead of describing, and choosing is often easier.

The other starts:

  • Ask. The AI builds its own idea of friendly. The owner sees it only when the page is done, and a change means building again.
  • Write. You would write sentences like "warm and clean". Nobody can check them, and ten people would draw ten different pages.
  • Write and draw. You would also write rules, but this page has few. The drawings do the work.

Job 4: A booking tool for a dental clinic

A dental clinic wants a booking tool for its website. Patients pick a time and leave a name and phone number.

  • Patients will judge the screens, so it must be clear what to press.
  • It keeps personal details, so who may see them matters.
  • Rules matter too. Two people could pick the same time, so the tool needs a rule for that.

How do you start? Ask, Write, Draw or Write and draw.

Show the best start

Best start: Write and draw. You write the rules, draw the screens and keep both. Whoever builds it then has the rules for what it does and the drawings for how it looks.

Why: It has rules that matter and screens that people judge. Either one alone would leave a gap, so you use both.

The other starts:

  • Ask. The AI decides what is saved and who can see it. Nobody notices until it matters.
  • Write. The rules come out right, but the booking screen may confuse patients, and words cannot show you that.
  • Draw. The screens look right, but nobody wrote down what is saved, who can see it or what happens when a time is taken.

Job 5: A drawing and a one-line task

A designer gives you a drawing of three screens and a one-line task: "Let customers change the date of an order." You want an AI to help plan the work.

  • The screens already exist, so there is nothing left to draw.
  • The task does not say what happens if the order has already shipped.
  • A drawing shows what happens when everything goes well.

How do you start? Ask, Write, Draw or Write and draw.

Show the best start

Best start: Write. You give the AI the drawing and the task and ask it to interview you about what they leave out. The answers become rules you can check, and you keep them with the drawing.

Why: The drawing shows the normal path. The rules for what goes wrong are not written yet. Tuomas Härkönen, a senior product designer writing on the Futurice blog, makes a similar point: screens alone do not tell the builders enough.

The other starts:

  • Ask. The AI builds from the drawing and guesses the rest: orders already shipped, a date in the past, two changes in a row. You find the gaps when someone runs into them.
  • Draw. The drawing already exists. Drawing again adds nothing, because the open questions are about rules, not looks.
  • Write and draw. You would draw the screens again, and they already exist. The written rules are what is missing.
Takeaway

Ask when the job is small and has one clear right result that you can check at a glance.
Write when the job has rules that matter.
Draw when people will judge it by looking.
Do both when it has both.

Step 3 · Your own job

Now try it on a job of your own

Think of something you want an AI to build or change for you. Up to three yes or no questions show where to start.

Answer yes or no

Question 1. Is it a small job with one clear right result, which you can check at a glance? Tidying a list of dates is a yes. A home page or a booking tool is a no. If yes, your start is Ask. If no, use the table below.

Question 2. Will people judge the result by looking at it? For example, how a page is laid out, or what comes first on it.

Question 3. Does it have rules that matter, such as sums, money, saved details or who may see what?

Your start, if your answer to question 1 was no
Rules matter: noRules matter: yes
Judged by looking: noAsk, and read what it didWrite
Judged by looking: yesDrawWrite and draw
  • Ask. Describe it in a chat and look at what comes back. If it comes back wrong, say so and ask again.
  • Ask, and read what it did. Start by asking. If the AI makes choices you did not expect, that is your sign to write a short spec.
  • Write. Write down the rules before anything is built. Let the AI interview you, then keep the short list it writes. Read the guide: Write It Down Before You Build It.
  • Draw. Look at rough drawings before anything is built. Ask for three really different ones, pick one, then change it. Read the guide: Draw It Before You Build It. Drawing needs a tool that can show pages and take your comments. Check what yours offers and what it costs.
  • Write and draw. Do both. Write down the rules, draw the screens, then keep both together. Either can come first. Read Write It Down Before You Build It and Draw It Before You Build It. Drawing needs a tool that can show pages and take your comments. Check what yours offers and what it costs.
Takeaway

The wrong start costs you time. These questions are a rough guide, not a rule.
