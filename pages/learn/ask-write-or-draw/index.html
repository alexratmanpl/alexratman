A 5-minute scroll about starting a job with AI

Ask, write
or draw?

Before an AI builds anything for you, you can start in three ways. Ask in a chat. Write down what it must do, in a short list called a spec. Or draw what it must look like. The wrong start costs you time. This page helps you pick, with five short jobs to try.

Step 1 · Three ways to start

Every start has a price

The AI is the same in all of them. What changes is how much you decide before it builds, and how much it decides for you. When the choices matter, you want to make them. When they don't, deciding up front only wastes your time.

Explore

Press each start to see what it asks from you and what it gives back.

Ask. You describe the job in a chat and see what comes back.

  • You do Type what you want, in a sentence or two.
  • You get A first try within a minute.
  • Fits when The job is small and has one clear right result. You can say it in one sentence and check it at a glance.
  • Goes wrong when The AI has to guess. It fills every gap with its own choice, and you find out after it has built the thing.

Effort before you see a result: 1 of 5 · Rules you decide up front: 1 of 5 · Look you decide up front: 1 of 5

Takeaway: Fast, and fine for small jobs. The risk is whatever you did not say.

Write. Before anything is built, you and the AI write down what the thing must do, as plain numbered sentences. The list is called a spec.

  • You do Tell the AI your idea and answer its questions. Read the short list of rules it writes, and correct it.
  • You get Rules you can read, change and check.
  • Fits when The job has rules that matter, such as sums, saved data or who may see what, and you can check each rule.
  • Goes wrong when The job is so small that the writing costs more than the job. Or it is about how things look, and words cannot show that.

Effort before you see a result: 3 of 5 · Rules you decide up front: 5 of 5 · Look you decide up front: 1 of 5

Takeaway: Slower to start, but you decide the rules and can check each one.

Read the guide: Write It Down Before You Build It

Draw. You look at rough drawings of the screens, pick one and change it, before anything is built.

  • You do Ask for three really different drawings. Pick one in your own words, then comment on it.
  • You get A drawing you chose, and a written description of what to build.
  • Fits when People will judge the result by looking: layout, order, mood.
  • Goes wrong when There is nothing to look at, or the drawing promises something the content cannot deliver.

Effort before you see a result: 3 of 5 · Rules you decide up front: 2 of 5 · Look you decide up front: 5 of 5

Takeaway: Choosing between drawings is often easier than describing what you want. It costs rounds of looking and deciding.

Read the guide: Draw It Before You Build It

Write and draw. Many real jobs have rules and screens. Write down the rules, draw the screens, then keep both.

  • You do Do either first. Then use the other to fill in what the first one leaves out.
  • You get Rules you can check and screens you picked, kept together.
  • Fits when People judge how it looks, and it also handles something that matters, such as money or personal details.
  • Goes wrong when The job is small or little is at stake. Then Ask is enough.

Effort before you see a result: 5 of 5 · Rules you decide up front: 5 of 5 · Look you decide up front: 5 of 5

Takeaway: A drawing shows the path when all goes well. The written rules cover what happens when it does not.

Illustrative, not measured. The bars compare the starts with each other, not time or money.

"Ask" is not a lazy start. For a small job, it is the right one. The docs for Anthropic's coding tool point the same way: in most cases, a plain first prompt already does the right thing.

Drawing helps for another reason. People find it easier to recognise what they want than to describe it from nothing. Nielsen Norman Group, a usability research firm, states a general rule. Recognising something is easier than recalling it, because what you see gives your memory more clues.

Asking and writing work in any AI chat. Drawing needs a tool that can show pages and take your comments. Tools change fast, so check what yours offers and what it costs.

Step 2 · Five jobs

Pick the start, then say why

Use what you just read. For each job, choose how you would start and why, then press the button to see what would happen. Your first answer counts for the score. After that, try the other answers to see where they lead.

Your turn

Read each job. Pick how you would start, then pick the reason.

  1. Tidy a list of dates

    You have a list of 40 dates typed in different ways, such as 25 Oct 2026, October 26th 2026 and 27.10.2026. You want each one written as 2026-10-25.

    • You can say it in one sentence.
    • Each date has one clear right answer.
    • A wrong line would show at once.

    How do you start? Best answer: Ask. You paste the list and type the sentence. You look at the new list. If one line is wrong, you say so and the AI fixes it. This takes a few minutes.

    • Write. You spend half an hour on numbered rules for a job that takes one sentence to ask. The AI would do the same thing in the end.
    • Draw. There is nothing to look at. The AI would draw boxes around a list of dates.
    • Write and draw. You would write rules and draw screens for a job that has neither.

    Why? Best answer: It is small, and you can check the result at a glance. A wrong line shows at once and costs a minute to fix, so there is little to protect.

    • The AI is good with lists, so it will get every line right. It may not. With 40 lines, one could be wrong. Asking is safe here because you would spot a wrong line at once and could ask again.
    • Writing and drawing are only for big projects. Size is only part of it. A small job that handles people's details still needs its rules written down. Here, what makes asking safe is that you can check the result at a glance.

  2. Split a restaurant bill

    You want a small app that splits a restaurant bill between friends. It adds a tip and shows what each person pays.

    • It has rules: how the tip works, how to round, what to do with an empty box.
    • You can check each rule with a number: 100 shared by 4 people is 25.00 each.
    • The screen is only two boxes and a result.

    How do you start? Best answer: Write. The AI asks what it should do with the leftover cent. You decide, and each rule gets a check you can run yourself.

    • Ask. The AI picks the tip, the rounding and what to save, and tells you afterwards. You find choices you never made.
    • Draw. You could draw two boxes, but the screen is not the hard part. Nobody has decided the tip or the rounding.
    • Write and draw. You get rules and a drawing. For two boxes and a number, the drawing adds little.

    Why? Best answer: It has rules that matter, and you can check each one. Each rule is a sentence you can check with a number, so writing them down pays off.

    • People will judge it by how it looks. Not here. It is two boxes and a number. Drawing helps when looks are the question.
    • It is too big to ask for in one sentence. It is still a small job. The risk is that the AI would decide the rules for you, not the size.

    How the best start begins I want to build an app that splits a restaurant bill between friends. Interview me, one question at a time. Then write the spec as short numbered sentences.

  3. A home page for a bakery

    A small bakery needs a home page. The owner says: "Friendly and clean, with the loaf of the week on top." She cannot say more than that.

    • "Friendly" and "clean" fit many different pages, so there is no one right page.
    • There are few rules to test. The question is how it looks and what comes first.
    • The owner will judge the result by looking at it.

    How do you start? Best answer: Draw. You get three rough drawings that differ in what comes first. The owner points at one, and you start from there.

    • Ask. The AI builds its own idea of friendly. The owner sees it only when the page is done, and a change means building again.
    • Write. You would write sentences like "warm and clean". Nobody can check them, and ten people would draw ten different pages.
    • Write and draw. You would also write rules, but this page has few. The drawings do the work.

    Why? Best answer: People will judge it by looking, and words fit many different pages. A drawing lets the owner choose instead of describe, and choosing is often easier.

    • She will see the result at a glance, so asking is enough. She will see it, yes. But there is no one right page to check it against. She finds out what she wants only after you have built one, and a change means building again.
    • A home page is too big to ask for in one go. Size is not the problem. A plain page is easy to build. The hard part is agreeing what it should look like.

    How the best start begins Draw three really different home pages for a small bakery. They must differ in what comes first on the page. Make them rough drawings only, sized for a phone. Then wait for my pick.

  4. A booking tool for a dental clinic

    A dental clinic wants a booking tool for its website. Patients pick a time and leave a name and phone number. The clinic gets a list of reminders.

    • Patients will judge the screens, so it must be clear what to press.
    • It keeps personal details, so who may see them matters.
    • Rules matter too. Two people could pick the same time, so the tool needs a rule for that.

    How do you start? Best answer: Write and draw. You write the rules, draw the screens and keep both. The builders then have the rules for what it does and the drawings for how it looks.

    • Ask. The AI decides what is saved and who can see it. Nobody notices until it matters.
    • Write. The rules come out right, but the booking screen may confuse patients, and words cannot show you that.
    • Draw. The screens look right, but nobody wrote down what is saved, who can see it or what happens when a time is taken.

    Why? Best answer: It has rules that matter and screens that people judge. Either guide alone would leave a gap, so you use both.

    • Patients will judge it by looking, so drawings are enough. Drawings show the path when all goes well. They do not say what is saved, who sees it or what happens when two people pick the same time.
    • It is a big job, and big jobs always need both. Size alone does not decide it. A big job with nothing to look at needs rules, not drawings.

    How the best start begins Interview me about what this booking tool must do and keep private, one question at a time. Then give me three rough drawings of the booking screen that differ in what comes first.

  5. A drawing and a one-line task

    A designer gives you a drawing of three screens and a one-line task: "Let customers change the date of an order." You want an AI to help plan the work.

    • The screens already exist, so there is nothing left to draw.
    • The task does not say what happens if the order has already shipped.
    • A drawing shows what happens when everything goes well.

    How do you start? Best answer: Write. You give the AI the drawing and the task and ask it to interview you about what they leave out. The answers become rules you can check, and you keep them with the drawing.

    • Ask. The AI builds from the drawing and guesses the rest: orders already shipped, a date in the past, two changes in a row. You find the gaps when someone hits them.
    • Draw. The drawing already exists. Drawing again adds nothing, because the open questions are about rules, not looks.
    • Write and draw. You would draw the screens again, and they already exist. What is missing is the written rules.

    Why? Best answer: The drawing shows the normal path. The rules for what goes wrong are not written yet. Tuomas Härkönen, a senior product designer writing on the Futurice blog, makes a similar point: screens alone do not tell the builders enough.

    • The designer did the hard part, so the AI can build straight from the drawing. The drawing shows how it looks when all goes well. Nobody has said what happens to an order that has already shipped, so the AI would have to guess.
    • A one-line task is too short to be useful. A short task is a fine start. What matters is what nobody has written down yet.

    How the best start begins Here are three screens and a task. First, list what you can see as short numbered sentences. Then interview me, one question at a time, about what the screens do not show, such as what happens when something goes wrong.

The rule of thumb Ask when the job is small and has one clear right result that you can check at a glance. Write when the job has rules that matter. Draw when people will judge it by looking. Do both when it has both.

Step 3 · Your own job

Now try it on a job of your own

Think of something you want an AI to build or change for you. Up to three yes or no questions show where to start. It is a rough guide, not a rule.

Your own job

Think of a job you have in mind. Answer up to three questions.

Start at question 1.

  1. Is it a small job with one clear right result, which you can check at a glance? Tidying a list of dates is a yes. A home page or a booking tool is a no. If yes, your start is Ask. If no, go to question 2.
  2. Will people judge the result by looking at it? For example, how a page is laid out, or what comes first on it. If yes, go to question 3. If no, go to question 4.
  3. Does it also have rules that matter, such as sums, money, saved details or who may see what? If yes, your start is Write and draw. If no, your start is Draw.
  4. Does it have rules that matter, such as sums, money, saved details or who may see what? If yes, your start is Write. If no, your start is Ask, and read what it did.
  • Ask. Describe it in a chat and look at what comes back. If it comes back wrong, say so and ask again.
  • Ask, and read what it did. Start by asking. If the AI makes choices you did not expect, that is your sign to write a short spec.
  • Write. Write down the rules before anything is built. Let the AI interview you, then keep the short list it writes. Read the guide: Write It Down Before You Build It
  • Draw. Look at rough drawings before anything is built. Ask for three really different ones, pick one, then change it. Read the guide: Draw It Before You Build It
  • Write and draw. Do both. Write down the rules, draw the screens, then keep both together. Either can come first. Read Write It Down Before You Build It and Draw It Before You Build It.

Each guide below can be read on its own. They follow two small examples: a bill splitter and a bakery's home page.
