A 10-minute scroll about design-first work with AI

Draw it
before you build it

Some things are hard to say in words: how a page looks, where the buttons go, what comes first. "Design-first" means you look at rough drawings, pick one, change it, and only then let an AI build it. This page follows a pretend bakery. Short questions along the way let you try each idea. Want to try it right away? Go to step 7. Not sure a drawing suits your job? Check the quick guide first.

Step 1 · What it is

Words fit many pages. A drawing fits one.

Picture a baker who says: "Friendly and clean, with the loaf of the week on top." Ten designers would draw ten different pages. An AI would draw an eleventh.

For things you can count, words work well. "The total shows cents" is easy to check. "Friendly" is not. The AI fills that gap with its own taste, and you see the result only after the whole page is built.

An architect works the other way round. Nobody asks for a house in one sentence and then waits for the walls. You look at sketches, say "bigger kitchen", and only then do the builders start. Moving a line on paper takes a minute. Moving a wall takes weeks.

Design-first is that habit for software. Before anything is built, you look at rough drawings of a screen. Each one is a board: boxes and plain text, with no finished look. Boards are quick to change. Nielsen Norman Group, a usability research firm, adds that people may feel freer to criticise them.

People also find it easier to recognise what they want than to describe it from nothing. Nielsen Norman Group states a general rule: recognising something is easier than recalling it, because what you see gives your memory more clues. Its article is about screens and menus. Applying it to drawings is my own reading.

Here are three boards for the sentence above, for a pretend bakery called Crumb & Co. Each board is a different direction: a different idea of what the page leads with. Press A, B and C to look at each one. Every name, price and photo on this page is invented.

Direction

Your turn

Read each sentence. Pick what you do with it, then pick why.

  1. Friendly and clean

    You tell the AI: "Friendly and clean, with the loaf of the week on top." Boards A, B and C above all follow that sentence.

    • The three boards look very different.
    • The sentence does not say which one you want.
    • Nothing is built yet.

    What do you do? Best answer: Ask for a few rough drawings that differ, then pick one. You see real choices. Picking is often easier than describing, and a change is cheap while it is still a drawing.

    • Ask for the finished page. The AI picks one of the many pages that fit. You see it only when it is built, and a change now costs more.
    • Write a longer sentence. It narrows the choice a little, but you still cannot see the page. Words such as "friendly" stay open to taste.

    Why? Best answer: Words fit many pages. A drawing shows one you can judge. A sentence can be true for pages that feel very different. A drawing lets you judge at a glance.

    • The AI already knows what you mean by "friendly". It knows what many people mean, not what you mean. Boards A, B and C are all friendly to someone.
    • Drawings are always quicker than writing. Not always. A sentence is quicker to type. The gain is that you can judge a drawing at a glance.

    What you could send Draw three really different directions for the bakery home page. They must differ in what comes first. Draw them as rough boards.

  2. A rule you can check

    You also want this on the page: "Prices always show two decimals, like 4.50."

    • You can check it with one look at any price.
    • It holds, or it does not.
    • It does not depend on how the page looks.

    What do you do? Best answer: Write it down as a rule, and let every drawing follow it. A rule you can check belongs in words. Every board then shows prices the same way.

    • Ask for three drawings of it. Three drawings of one rule are three copies of the same thing. There is nothing to choose between.
    • Wait and see what the AI does. It may show 4.5, or 4.50, or something else. You find out only when the page is built.

    Why? Best answer: You can say it exactly, so there is nothing to choose. A sentence is the shortest way to say it, and a check is easy.

    • Seeing a price on a board tells you more than a rule. A board shows one price. The rule covers every price, and one look at the page checks it.
    • Rules belong to the builders, not to the design. A board with wrong prices promises something the shop cannot supply. Say the rule before anything is drawn.

    What you could send Rule: prices always show two decimals, like 4.50. Every board must follow it.

  3. Easy to order

    You write: "Ordering must be easy."

    • Easy for a regular, or for a new visitor?
    • The sentence does not say where the button goes.
    • It does not say how big it is.

    What do you do? Best answer: Ask for drawings that put ordering in different places. You see where the button could sit, and you can tell at a glance which place feels easy.

    • Ask the AI to make it easy. "Easy" is a feeling. The AI picks a place, and you see it only when it is built.
    • Write down a button size in pixels. A minimum size is a good rule to write down. But "easy to order" is also about where the button sits, and a number cannot show that. A board can.

    Why? Best answer: Where something sits, and how it feels, is judged by looking. That is what a board is for. You look, say what works, and the AI redraws.

    • Words can describe a button well enough. You can describe a button exactly, but "easy to order" is a feeling. A board shows you at a glance whether it feels easy.
    • A drawing of a button is too rough to judge. A rough box with a label is enough to judge where the button sits and how easy it is to find. Fine detail can wait.

    What you could send Draw three boards. In each one, ordering sits somewhere different. Say in one line what each choice puts first.

The rule of thumb Draw what people will judge by looking. Write down what you can check in words. Most jobs need both.

One word, two meanings. In some spec tools, such as Kiro, "design" is a technical plan written in text. Here it means what people see and touch: the screens.

Step 2 · Set the limits

First, say what is fixed and what is open

The whole method is one loop with a stop sign. The three red stations repeat for as long as you like. Then you freeze the result, and only then does anything get built.

The design-first loop
  1. 1
    Starting brief You say what the page is for, what is fixed and what is open.
  2. 2
    Directions The AI draws three really different directions as rough boards.
  3. 3
    Pick You choose one, in your own words.
  4. 4
    Refine You comment on spots. The AI redraws as version 2, 3, and so on.
  5. 5
    Freeze You say "this is it". The AI writes a build brief from the final boards.
  6. 6
    Build The real thing is built from the build brief.
  7. 7
    Check You, or an AI, verify the result against the build brief and the boards. A person decides whether it goes live.

Red stations 2 to 4 repeat until you are happy.

Station 1 sets the limits. Try it on the pretend bakery.

Your turn

Your first message is the starting brief. It splits the job in two. Fixed is what the AI must not touch: approved words, the brand colour, the pages that must exist. Open is what you want to see different ideas for: layout, order, mood. Without that split, the AI may redesign what you wanted left alone. If everything is fixed, you get three near-identical drawings. Read each item for the bakery. Is it fixed, or open?

  1. The shop name, Crumb & Co., and its red logo Fixed. It is approved. A new drawing should keep it as it is.
  2. What sits at the top of the home page Open. This is the kind of choice you want to see different ideas for.
  3. The words "Order by Thursday" Fixed. These are approved words. A redraw should keep them word for word.
  4. How busy or calm the page feels Open. The mood is something you judge by looking.
  5. Prices always show two decimals, like 4.50 Fixed. It is a rule you can check. Every drawing should follow it.
  6. Whether ordering is a big button, a bar or a link Open. Where and how people order is something you want different ideas for.

A quick test: what is already decided, or can be checked against a rule, is usually fixed. What you judge by looking is usually open.

Two more things go in the starting brief. The first is what one item of content looks like, so a drawing stays honest. A loaf has a name, a price and maybe a photo, but no nutrition table. The second is any rules ("prices always show two decimals") and words the page must never use, such as claims you cannot prove.

You may not know what you want until you are asked. So let the AI interview you. One rule makes it work: one question at a time, each with its reason. "I don't know yet, show me options" is a good answer. Twenty questions at once tend to get twenty shallow answers.

Step 3 · Directions

Ask for three really different directions

Ask for three directions that differ in what comes first, not three colour schemes of one layout. For the bakery, try these three. Photo first has a big photo with the loaf name on top. Order first is a compact list, with an order bar that stays on screen. Story first has no photo, big letters and a lot of white space.

Draw them as rough boards at phone size first, then check a larger screen. Keep the boards rough: fine detail can pull the talk towards colours and away from structure.

Your turn

Read each job. Pick the message that gets three really different directions.

  1. The bakery home page

    You want different ideas for the bakery home page. The name and logo are fixed. The AI will draw rough boards at phone size.

    • You want to compare ideas, not only colours.
    • Nothing is built yet.

    Which message do you send? Best answer: Draw three directions. Each must put something different first on the page. You get boards that differ in structure: perhaps one led by the photo, one by the order button and one by the story.

    • Show me three colour schemes for this page. You get one layout in three colours. You cannot compare ideas, and colour is the easy part to change later.
    • Draw the best page you can. You get one page. You can say yes or no, but you never see the other ways.

    What you could send Draw three really different directions, not three colour schemes. They must differ in what comes first on the page.

  2. A booking page

    A dental clinic wants a page where patients book a visit.

    • Patients might start from the date, the dentist or the treatment.
    • The clinic cannot tell which suits its patients.

    Which message do you send? Best answer: Draw three booking pages. One starts with the date, one with the dentist, one with the treatment. Each board asks the patient something different first. Now you can see which order feels natural.

    • Draw three versions with different fonts. Fonts change the look, not what comes first. The three pages work the same way.
    • Draw one booking page, then draw it twice more. The second and third drawings copy the first. You get three look-alikes.

    What you could send Draw three booking pages. One starts with the date, one with the dentist and one with the treatment. Keep them rough.

  3. Two look-alikes

    The AI drew three boards for the bakery. Boards A and B differ only in colour.

    • Board C is different.
    • You wanted three real directions.

    Which message do you send? Best answer: Say: "A and B only differ in colour. Redo B so something else leads." The AI knows what is wrong and what to change. You get a new direction for B.

    • Pick the one with the nicest colours. You choose between paint jobs, and the structure stays the same.
    • Say: "Make them more different." It is not clear what to change. The AI may change the wrong thing.

    What you could send A and B only differ in colour. Redo B so something else leads on the page.

The rule of thumb Ask for directions that differ in what comes first, not in colour. If two look alike, say which two and what should change.

Three is a good number. Nielsen Norman Group suggests making about three to five quick, rough alternatives, then combining their best parts. In one lab study, 33 people designed banner ads. Some made several designs before they got any feedback. Others made one design at a time, with feedback after each one. Experts rated the ads of the first group higher.

A drawing can promise anything. So ask for three marks on every board, and a legend that explains them. No tag means it works today. LATER means it needs something new first. Square brackets mean the text is a placeholder, not a fact. The boards below use these marks. Look at the LATER items on each one.

References help too. Words like "warm" mean little; sites you can point at mean a lot. Ask the AI for about six reference sites. Choose up to three and say what you like about each, such as "the colours". Name one or two "not like this". Ask which sites it opened, because an AI may describe a site from memory. Copy qualities, not pages.

Step 4 · Pick and refine

Pick in your own words, then change it in batches

Do not answer "B". Say why: "B, because the order button is the first thing I see. But I like A's big photo." You may mix: "B's top, A's cards". Your reasons are what the AI works from.

When you ask "what do you think?", a good AI gives two or three options and a recommendation. You can then agree or push back, instead of inventing an answer. Before you choose, check the LATER tags. The prettiest board may promise what the content cannot supply.

Now the loop turns. Many design tools let you pin a comment to a spot, like a sticky note on a drawing. Leave all your comments first. Then say "that's all", so the AI redraws once for the whole batch. Without pinned comments, write them as one numbered list.

Ask the AI to save each redraw as a new version, so you can go back. Some tools do not keep versions for you. Ask for a short decision log too: one line per decision, with the reason.

Your turn

Read each job. Pick the reply that helps the AI most.

  1. Picking a direction

    Three boards are on screen. You like the order button on B and the big photo on A.

    • The AI is waiting for your pick.
    • Your reasons are what it works from.

    Which reply do you send? Best answer: B, because the order button is the first thing I see. But I like A's big photo. Now the AI knows what to keep and what to borrow. It can mix B's top with A's photo.

    • B. It tells the AI which board, but not why. It cannot tell what you liked about B.
    • Make it nicer. "Nicer" means different things to different people. The AI has to guess.

    What you could send B, because the order button is the first thing I see. But I like A's big photo.

  2. Five changes

    You want five changes to board B: bigger type, a photo on top, calmer colours, a row of tags and shorter text.

    • The AI redraws each time you ask.
    • Each redraw is one round of asking and checking.

    Which reply do you send? Best answer: Write all five comments first. Then say "that's all", so the AI redraws once. One round and one new version, and the AI can fit the changes together.

    • Send each change as its own message, and wait for each redraw. That is five rounds. Each round takes time, and a later change can undo an earlier one.
    • Say "improve the design" and see what happens. You give up control of what changes. The AI decides.

    What you could send My comments on B: 1. Bigger type. 2. A photo on top. 3. Calmer colours. 4. A row of tags. 5. Shorter text. That's all. Please redraw once, as a new version.

  3. A surprise in version 3

    Version 3 looks different from version 2 in a place you did not mention. You are not sure what changed.

    • You approved the top of the page in version 2.
    • The AI did not say what it changed.

    Which reply do you send? Best answer: Ask: "List everything that changed in this version." The AI lists each change, and you can say which ones to undo.

    • Say nothing. It looks fine. A redraw can quietly change something you had approved. If you do not ask, you may never notice.
    • Start again from the first board. You throw away work that was fine. Ask what changed first.

    What you could send List everything that changed in version 3. Tell me which of my approved choices moved.

The rule of thumb Give reasons when you pick. Collect your comments and send them together. After each redraw, ask what changed.

Step 5 · Test, then freeze

Look at it rendered, then freeze it

"Looks right in the code" is not "looks right". Boards are made of code, the instructions a browser follows to draw a page. An AI can read that code and believe the page is fine without ever drawing it. So look at the boards rendered: drawn on a screen, the way a visitor sees them.

Start on a phone, then check a desktop. Ask for the awkward cases too: no photo, a very long name, nothing found, an error. Then ask: "Did you look at this rendered, or did you reason about it? Show me the evidence." Some chat tools only write code and cannot show a screenshot. If yours is one, open the file in your own browser and look yourself.

Your turn

Read what the AI says. Pick what it did, then pick what you do next.

  1. Looks good

    The AI says: "I checked all three boards. They look good at phone size and at desktop size."

    • It names no screenshot or width.
    • It does not say whether it opened the boards.

    What did the AI do? Best answer: I can't tell. The message gives no evidence either way. That is exactly why you ask.

    • It looked. Nothing in the message shows it. "I checked" could mean it opened them, or only read the code.
    • It only reasoned. It may have. The message does not say, so you cannot tell.

    What do you do next? Best answer: Ask: "Did you look at this rendered, or did you reason about it? Show me the evidence." One question separates the two. A real check comes with a screenshot or a list of what it saw.

    • Accept it. The AI said it checked. "Checked" can mean it read code that looked fine. A board can still be wrong when it is drawn.
    • Ask it to say it again, more clearly. It will say the same words. You need evidence, not a repeat.

    What you could send Did you look at the boards rendered, or did you reason about them? Show me the evidence: a screenshot for each board at phone width.

  2. With screenshots

    The AI says: "I opened each board in a browser at 390 and 1280 pixels wide, and saved screenshots. On board C the loaf name wraps onto three lines on the phone. I have not fixed it yet."

    • It names the widths.
    • It saved screenshots.
    • It reports a problem.

    What did the AI do? Best answer: It looked. It opened the boards, named the widths and reported what it saw. You can check its screenshots.

    • It only reasoned. A guess from code does not come with screenshots or a problem it found on one board.
    • I can't tell. You can tell here: the message names the widths, mentions screenshots and reports one specific fault.

    What do you do next? Best answer: Open the screenshots, see the wrapping, and decide what to change. You have evidence to judge. Your decision is what is needed now.

    • Ask it to check again. It already showed its evidence. Asking again only costs time.
    • Ignore it. A name that long will never happen. Real names come in all lengths. Ask for the awkward cases on purpose.

    What you could send Fix the loaf name on board C so it fits on two lines at phone width. Show me a new screenshot.

  3. It should fit

    The AI says: "The code uses a flexible width, so the loaf name will fit on any phone."

    • It talks about the code.
    • It does not mention a screenshot.
    • It says what "will" happen.

    What did the AI do? Best answer: It only reasoned. It read the code and drew a conclusion. That is a guess about the page, not a look at it.

    • It looked. It describes the code, not a page it saw. No screenshot is mentioned.
    • I can't tell. You can tell here: the message talks about what the code should do, and shows nothing the AI saw.

    What do you do next? Best answer: Ask for a rendered check at phone width, with a very long loaf name. Now you see what happens in the awkward case, with evidence.

    • Trust it. A flexible width always fits. It often does, but not always. A very long word can still spill out of its box.
    • Ask it to explain the code. You get more reasoning. You still have not seen the page.

    What you could send Show me board A rendered at phone width, with the loaf name "Rye & caraway with extra seeded crust". Give me a screenshot.

The rule of thumb Ask every time: did you run it, or imagine it? A screenshot, a test result or a file list is evidence. "It should work" is not.

When you say "this is it", the design stops moving. That is the freeze. The AI turns the final boards into a build brief: a written, exact description of what to build. The boards show how it looks. The build brief says what to build.

A build brief holds
  1. Named values. Exact values with a name, such as crust-brown for one colour. Designers call them tokens.
  2. Page structure. What sits where, from top to bottom.
  3. Components. Reusable pieces, such as a button or a loaf card.
  4. States. What shows with no photo, a very long name, nothing found, or an error.
  5. Accessibility notes. What people with different abilities and settings need.
  6. Open items. Which parts are placeholders or LATER.

Read the build brief against the boards. Ask the AI to list every place where they could disagree, and decide in advance which one wins. I suggest the boards. You now have a frozen design and a written build brief, not a live website. Hand both to whoever builds it.

Step 6 · After the freeze

The build brief is the contract. People still decide.

Once the design is frozen, the build brief says what to build, and the boards show how it should look. "Done" becomes something you can check.

Before anything goes live, verify the result. You can do it yourself, or ask an AI in a new chat. If an AI checks, give it the build brief and the boards, not your own verdict. Ask it to report only where the result departs from the build brief or the boards. The docs for Claude Code, Anthropic's coding tool, say a fresh reviewer helps, because it has not seen your reasoning.

The good and the bad

  • +

    Choosing is often easier than describing

    You react to real pictures, instead of finding the words yourself.

  • +

    Rough work is cheap to change

    A sketch can be redrawn in minutes, before anyone builds on it.

  • +

    Builders get a target

    Everyone builds and checks against the same boards and the same build brief.

  • −

    It costs rounds and time

    Looking, deciding and redrawing take turns, and each turn takes time.

  • −

    Many small choices tire you

    Send your comments in batches, and let the AI recommend.

  • −

    A drawing can promise too much

    A board can show what the content or the code cannot deliver, and a redraw can change something you had approved. The LATER tag and the question "what changed?" are your defences.

Skip drawing first for a change in the hidden part of a site, with nothing to look at. Skip it for a change you could describe in one sentence. Skip it too when the content is not known yet. If your project already has a design system, ask the AI to follow it. A design system is a written set of colours, type and parts that the whole product uses.

Step 7 · Try it yourself

See the loop once

This demo runs the loop on the pretend bakery. Pick a direction, add comments, redraw, and freeze it into a build brief. A real AI would redraw from your comments. This toy uses a fixed script, so it shows the rhythm, not the quality. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

  2. 2 Comment
  3. 3 Redraw
  4. 4 Freeze

1 Pick a direction

Each picture is the same "Loaf of the week" page, drawn three ways. Pick the one you would start from. It becomes Version 1.

Use the arrow keys to move between them. After your first redraw the direction is locked in; press Start again to pick another.

Nothing is thrown away: press any version to look at it again.

2 Comment

Press every change you want. Nothing is redrawn yet: the comments wait in a batch.

3 Redraw

One redraw applies every comment in the batch at once and makes a new version. The comments then clear, so you can start the next batch.

That is 8 versions, the most this demo keeps. Real projects stop too: at some point more versions stop helping, and it is time to freeze one.

    Where a drawing stops
    
    
    A drawing shows the normal path. It does not show the rules for errors, data or who may see what. Tuomas Härkönen, a senior product designer, makes a similar point on the Futurice blog. He writes: "A mockup shows the happy path while hiding the edge cases, error states and data consistency rules where production bugs live." So when a job has rules that matter, write them down too. Write It Down Before You Build It shows how.
    
    
    The idea is old. In 2006 Marty Cagan argued that most of a product spec should be a high-fidelity prototype: a detailed mock-up of the real thing. He called use cases, which describe the main flows through a product, a useful supplement.
