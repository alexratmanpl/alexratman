A 10-minute scroll about design-first work with AI

Draw it
before you build it

Some things are hard to say in words: how a screen looks, where the buttons go, what comes first. Design-first means you look at rough drawings, pick one, change it, and only then let an AI build it. This page follows a pretend bakery.

Try it right away in step 7. If you're not sure a drawing suits your job, read Ask, Write or Draw? first.

Step 1 · What it is

Words fit many pages. A drawing fits one.

Picture a baker who says: "Friendly and clean, with the loaf of the week on top." Ten designers would draw ten different pages. An AI would draw an eleventh. "Friendly" is not something you can check, so the AI fills the gap with its own taste. You see the result only after the whole thing is built.

The baker's sentence · a made-up example

Words

"Friendly and clean, with the loaf of the week on top."

and many more

Many pages fit these words.

A drawing

A drawing shows you one page you can judge before it is built.

A rough drawing of a page, with boxes and simple text, is called a board.

Takeaway

Draw what people will judge by looking. Write down what you can check in words. Most jobs need both.

Step 2 · The loop

One loop with a stop sign

The whole method is one loop. You set the limits, the AI draws three directions, and you pick one and refine it. Only when you freeze the result does anything get built.

The design-first loop
  1. 1
    Starting briefYou say what the page is for, what is fixed and what is open.
  2. 2
    DirectionsThe AI draws three really different directions as rough boards.
  3. 3
    PickYou choose one, in your own words.
  4. 4
    RefineYou comment on spots. The AI redraws as version 2, 3, and so on.
  5. 5
    FreezeYou say "this is it". The AI writes a build brief from the final boards.
  6. 6
    BuildThe real thing is built from the build brief.
  7. 7
    CheckYou, or an AI, check the result against the build brief and the boards. A person decides whether it goes live.
Takeaway

Stations 2 to 4 repeat until you are happy. Nothing is built before the freeze.

Step 3 · The starting brief

Say what is fixed and what is open

Your first message splits the job in two. Fixed is what the AI must not touch: approved words, the brand colour, the pages that must exist. Open is what you want different ideas for: layout, order, mood. If you're not sure yet, let the AI interview you: one question at a time, each with its reason.

The bakery's starting brief · a made-up example

Fixed

  • The shop name, Crumb & Co., and its red logo.
  • The words "Order by Thursday".
  • Prices always show two decimals, like 4.50.
  • What one loaf has: a name, a price, maybe a photo. No nutrition table.

Open

  • What sits at the top of the home page.
  • How busy or calm the page feels.
  • Whether ordering is a big button, a bar or a link.

A quick test: what is already decided, or can be checked against a rule, is usually fixed. What you judge by looking is usually open.

Takeaway

Without the split, the AI may redesign what you wanted left alone. With everything fixed, you get three near-identical drawings.

Step 4 · Directions

Ask for three really different directions

Ask for three directions that differ in what comes first, not three colour schemes of one layout. Draw them as rough boards, at phone size first. A drawing can promise anything, so ask the AI to tag what is not real yet. No tag means it works today. LATER means it needs something new first. Square brackets mean a placeholder.

Direction
Page
Size
  • no tag works today
  • LATER needs a new field or feature first
  • [square brackets] placeholder text, not a fact

Three is a good number. Nielsen Norman Group suggests making about three to five quick, rough alternatives, then combining their best parts.

Takeaway

If two boards look alike, say which two and what should change: "A and B only differ in colour. Redo B so something else leads."

Step 5 · Pick and refine

Pick in your own words, then change it in batches

Do not answer "B". Say why, and what to borrow from the others. Then leave all your comments first and say "that's all", so the AI redraws once for the whole batch. Ask it to save each redraw as a new version, and to keep a short decision log: one line per decision, with the reason.

Three messages you could send · the bakery
Pick
B, because the order button is the first thing I see. But I like A's big photo.
One batch
My comments on B:
  1. Bigger type.
  2. A photo on top.
  3. Calmer colours.
  4. A row of tags.
  5. Shorter text.
That's all. Please redraw once, as a new version.
After a redraw
List everything that changed in version 2. Tell me which of my approved choices moved.

A redraw can quietly change something you had approved. Before you choose, check the LATER tags too: the prettiest board may promise what the content cannot supply.

Takeaway

Give reasons when you pick. Collect your comments and send them together. After each redraw, ask what changed.

Step 6 · Test, then freeze

Look at it on a screen, then freeze it

Boards are made of code. An AI can read that code and tell you the page is fine without ever drawing it. So look at the boards on a real phone and a real desktop. Include the awkward cases: no photo, a very long name, an error. When you are happy, say "this is it". That is the freeze.

Step 7 · Try it yourself

See the loop once

Pick a direction, add comments, redraw, and freeze it into a build brief. A real AI would redraw from your comments. This toy uses a fixed script, so it shows the rhythm, not the quality. Nothing you do here is saved or sent anywhere.

1 Pick a direction

Each picture is the same "Loaf of the week" page, drawn three ways. Pick the one you would start from. It becomes Version 1.

Use the arrow keys to move between them. After your first redraw the direction is locked in; press Start again to pick another.

Nothing is thrown away: press any version to look at it again.

2 Comment

Press every change you want. Nothing is redrawn yet: the comments wait in a batch.

3 Redraw

One redraw applies every comment in the batch at once and makes a new version. The comments then clear, so you can start the next batch.

That is 8 versions, the most this demo keeps. Real projects stop too: at some point more versions stop helping, and it is time to freeze one.

    Where a drawing stops
    

    When to draw first, and when not
    

    In my reading of a Nielsen Norman Group guideline, choosing is often easier than describing. Rough work is cheap to change. But the loop costs rounds and time, many small choices can tire you, and a drawing can promise too much.
    

    Skip drawing first for a change in the hidden part of a site, with nothing to look at. Skip it for a change you could describe in one sentence, and when the content is not known yet.
    

    A drawing shows the normal path. It does not show the rules for errors, data or who may see what. Tuomas Härkönen, a senior product designer, makes a similar point on the Futurice blog. He writes: "A mockup shows the happy path while hiding the edge cases, error states and data consistency rules where production bugs live." So when a job has rules that matter, write them down too. Write It Down Before You Build It shows how.
