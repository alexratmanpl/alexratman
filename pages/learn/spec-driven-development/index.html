A 5-minute scroll about spec-driven development

Write it down
before you build it

"Spec-driven development" is a long name for a simple habit. Before an AI builds anything, you write down what you want, in plain sentences. This page follows one small example, a holiday-request form, then gives you simple steps and ready-made messages to try it yourself. It covers the downsides too. Want to start right away? Step 1 · What it is

First, write down what the app should do

Many people start by chatting with an AI: "Build me a form for holiday requests." The AI then makes lots of small decisions on its own. In this guide, an "app" means anything you build this way, such as a form, a calculator or a small website.

I learned how much that matters early on. One of my first tries was my own "about me" page. I told the AI only that I'm a software developer and that the page should look good, and I used a leading AI model. Wherever it found a gap, in the design or in my own background, it filled it with made-up details that weren't about me. People call this "hallucinating".

Spec-driven development changes the order. First you and the AI write down what the app must do, which leaves fewer gaps for the AI to fill with guesses. Then the AI builds from that description, and you check the result against it. The description is yours to read and fix.

This written description is called a spec, short for "specification". For the holiday form, it looks like this.

Holiday requests · what it must do
  1. An employee asks for time off by picking a first day and a last day.
  2. The last day can't be before the first day.
  3. The employee's manager can approve or refuse the request.
  4. The employee is told the answer.

A real spec would be longer. What matters is the style: plain words, one rule per line. You can check it without reading code, the instructions a computer follows.

To start, tell an AI chat your idea and ask it to write the spec as short numbered sentences. Step 7 gives you the exact words.

You don't need special software for this. A normal AI chat and a file of your own, such as a Word, Google Docs or Notepad file, are enough. Programmers do have tools built around the idea, such as Spec Kit and Kiro, but you can ignore them for now.

Step 2 · Building in stages

Then build it in small stages, and check each one

Don't ask the AI for the whole app at once. Ask for one stage at a time, and read what you get before you say "next". The tools from step 1, Spec Kit and Kiro, are both built around stages like these.

Changing a sentence is quick. Finding out later that the whole app is built on a wrong guess is slow. The pauses do add reading, though, and I come back to that in step 6.

Step 3 · Safety and watching

Write down the safety rules, and who is told when something breaks

If a spec says nothing about who is allowed to see what, the AI picks something. Sometimes that's wrong, and nobody notices until it matters.

A real case shows the risk. Moltbook is a social website. Its founder said he built it with AI and "didn't write a single line of code". In February 2026 the security company Wiz reported that anyone could read and change the information the site stored, including about 35,000 email addresses. The site's access rules, which say who is allowed to see or change what, hadn't been set up properly. The Moltbook team secured it within hours, with Wiz's help.

I'm not saying a spec would have prevented that. Wiz adds that today's AI tools don't yet think about security or about who gets access, so the settings still benefit from a careful human check. You can do that check yourself: a spec gives you somewhere to write the rules down, so there's something to check. Try it below.

Add lines to the holiday spec
  • Lines 1–4The four lines from step 1.
  • Line 5Not written down: who is allowed to see which request. The AI picks for itself, and nothing checks it.
  • Line 6Not written down: who to warn when something breaks. If saving breaks, the app may fail without telling anyone.

A line you write down can be checked later, and a rule you leave out can't.

You don't have to invent the safety questions. In June 2026 Cloudflare, a company that protects websites from attacks, released a free security-audit skill. A skill is a ready-made set of instructions for an AI tool, and this one makes an AI act as a security auditor. I took four questions from its checklist and put them in my own words. I think they suit any spec:

Four safety questions to ask your AI about any spec
  1. Who is allowed to see or change what? Being signed in doesn't mean being allowed to see everything.
  2. Where are passwords and other secret codes kept? Not written into the app itself.
  3. Is any shortcut you used while building, such as a login that lets anyone in, still open once people use the app?
  4. When something goes wrong, does the app show technical details that could help someone break in?

Paste these four questions into your AI chat with your spec, and ask it to answer each one. Then read the answers yourself, and ask the AI to explain anything you don't follow. The skill itself needs software set up for programmers, so step 7 only points to it.

The second button in the "Add lines" box was about watching the app once people use it. Engineers call this monitoring: you hear about trouble early, and can see enough to find the cause. To write your own line, start with what the people using it care about. Here, they care that their request is saved, so the line says who is told when saving fails. That can be you. Google's engineers give the same advice: start from what users care about, not from what you can measure.

Step 4 · When something breaks

When something breaks, a spec helps you start again, but it can't bring back your data

Apps do break: a change goes wrong, or someone deletes the wrong file. So save your spec, your plan and your checks as files you can find again (the next step explains checks). A fresh AI chat can then read them and try to build the app again.

A new chat usually starts from nothing, so everything you explained in the old one is gone.

With spec-driven development

You give the saved spec to a new chat, and it continues from there. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, gives similar advice: once the spec is complete, start a fresh chat to build from it.

What else you need

The spec saved as a file you can find again, not only inside the chat.

Don't expect a perfect copy. An AI can answer the same question differently each time, and the same goes for a program built from one spec. Birgitta Böckeler, an engineer at the technology company Thoughtworks, looked at three spec tools. With one of them she had the AI write the code several times from the same spec and saw the results vary. So run the same checks on the rebuilt app as on the first.

Your data needs its own backup, because the spec holds none of the requests people have already sent. For a small tool, ask the AI to add a button that saves all the requests as a spreadsheet file. Press it daily if losing a day of requests would hurt. Keep the file in two places. One is an online folder you already use, such as Google Drive. The other is a USB stick or external drive. Unplug it afterwards, because some viruses reach devices left connected to an infected computer. Then open the file once, to check it holds everything. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre gives similar advice. The file holds people's details, so keep both copies private, and check your workplace's rules on keeping them.

Step 5 · Checking the work

Turn each line of the spec into a check

A check (engineers say "test") looks at one line of the spec and ends in pass or fail. Ask the AI to write one check for every line, as a plain sentence like the ones below. Your job is to read each one and ask: does this say what I meant? Then try it yourself on the finished app and see whether it passes. Later you can ask the AI to make the computer run the checks for you.

Four of the holiday form's six lines are shown below, each with its check. Press the button to run them. It's a made-up run.

Four spec lines, four checks
  • Line 2SpecThe last day can't be before the first day.CheckPick a last day before the first. The app refuses it.Not run
  • Line 3SpecThe manager can approve or refuse the request.CheckThe manager approves one request and refuses another. Each shows the right answer.Not run
  • Line 5SpecOnly the employee and their manager can open a request.CheckSigned in as Ana, try to open Ben's request. She isn't Ben or his manager, so the app must refuse.Ana could read Ben's request.Not run
  • Line 6SpecIf saving fails again and again, the person who looks after the app is told.CheckMake saving fail again and again. The person who looks after the app is told.Not run
Not run yet.

Passing every check is a good sign, but only for the lines that were written down.

Checks look at one line each. A review is different: a fresh AI chat, or you, reads all the work and compares it with the spec. To get one, open a new chat, give it your spec and the finished work, and ask what's missing or different. Step 7 has the words.

A new chat is better, because it didn't write the work. The docs for Claude Code, Anthropic's coding tool, say the same. Ask more than once, too. In Cloudflare's tests of a security checker for code, one run found only about half of the problems that repeated runs found. A chat may miss things in the same way.

A check is worth less if the AI can change it. When Anthropic launched its AI model Claude 3.7 Sonnet in February 2025, it published a report on it. The report says that in coding tools like Claude Code, the model occasionally took shortcuts to pass tests (the same thing as checks). Most often it gave back the exact answers the tests expected, instead of writing code that works in general. That is like copying the answer from the back of the book instead of working it out. The model also changed tests to match its code. Anthropic says it added partial fixes before launch, and that they significantly reduced how often this happened in typical use. So if an AI changes a check, ask why.

Step 6 · The good and the bad

What's good about it, and what isn't

Here is what people have reported, for and against. Use it to decide how much spec your own project needs. Step 7 helps you choose.

Good points
  • +

    It tackles a common problem: things nobody wrote down

    In a 2014–15 survey of 228 companies, 48% ranked incomplete or hidden requirements among their five most critical problems. The survey was about software projects in general, not about specs. Hidden requirements are things the software must do that nobody wrote down. A spec is one way to put them in writing, in plain words that you and the AI can both read.

  • +

    Written notes help an AI pick up where it left off

    Anthropic's engineering blog describes AI programs that work on one project over many sessions (separate runs). It says "each new session begins with no memory of what came before". Part of its fix is written notes: a progress file and a feature list that the next session reads at the start. A saved spec is the same kind of note.

  • +

    A review can name the requirement behind a problem

    In one small trial, five reviewers each reviewed AI-generated banking software twice: once with the approved spec and design documents, and once without them. With the spec, 81% of what they found could be tied to a named requirement. Without it, none could. They found about the same share of the known problems either way, so the spec did not make them better at finding problems. The author says to read the result as a strong early signal, not as proof.

Problems
  • −

    More to read, and too much for small fixes

    Böckeler asked Kiro to fix a small bug (a mistake in a program). It turned that one bug into a long list of requirements: four "user stories" and sixteen "acceptance criteria", meaning conditions the fix had to meet. She said the workflow was like "a sledgehammer to crack a nut": far too heavy for a small job. Kiro has since added a spec type for bug fixes. In the trial above, reviews took about 22 minutes longer with the spec and design documents.

  • −

    Plans change, and the spec goes out of date

    Programmer Kent Beck, quoted on Martin Fowler's site, says the descriptions of spec-driven development he has seen stress writing the whole spec first. To him, it is "bizarre" to assume that you won't learn anything while building that would change the spec. A Thoughtworks writer who supports the approach says "spec drift" and hallucination are "inherently difficult to avoid". By spec drift I mean the spec and the app slowly no longer matching.

  • −

    The AI may not follow it

    In one try in September 2025, Böckeler asked Spec Kit to build on existing code. The AI ignored the notes saying parts of it already existed and wrote them all again. She notes that the tools may have changed since.

Step 7 · Try it yourself

Now try it on something real

The holiday form was a teaching example. For your first real try, pick one small idea of your own. It should work on your own computer and hold no one else's details, like a packing checklist or a countdown to a trip. This guide uses a page that splits a restaurant bill between friends.

You'll need a laptop with a web browser, a free AI chat such as Claude or ChatGPT, and Notepad (Windows) or TextEdit (Mac). Set aside a couple of hours.

Here is the spec for the bill splitter. A spec is a short numbered list of what the page must do.

Bill splitter · what it must do
  1. I type the total bill and the number of people.
  2. The page shows what each person pays, with cents, like 12.50.
  3. I can add a tip as a percentage. The page then shows the new total and each person's share.
  4. If a box is empty, or the number of people is zero, the page shows a short message instead of a result.
  5. The page doesn't save or send anything I type. When I close it, everything is gone.
  6. It still looks fine when I make the browser window narrow, like a phone screen.

Each line also gets a check, a plain sentence that tests it, such as "Type 100 and 4 people. The page shows 25.00 each." Two short lists follow: what to avoid, and what to check.

For your first try
Avoid
  • Putting other people's details, or any passwords or secret codes, into a chat. Use made-up ones while you test.
  • Asking for the whole page at once. Work one task at a time.
  • Changing what the page does without changing the spec. Update the spec first.
  • Letting the AI change a check without asking why.
  • Following steps that ask you to install software or type commands (typed lines in a special window). Ask the AI for an easier way.
  • Showing the page to other people before it passes your checks.
Check
  • After every reply from the AI: is this what I meant?
  • Does every line of your spec have a check, and do the checks pass?
  • Have you asked the AI the safety questions? Part 2 does this.
  • Has a new chat compared the finished page with your spec? Part 5 does this.
  • If your page saves anything, have you opened your backup file once?

What you do next

Answer in your own words. It's fine to say "I'm not sure". When you have answered enough, say "That's enough, please write the spec." You get a spec like the one above. For bigger ideas, Anthropic advises letting the AI interview you first.

Part 4 gives you the page, and the AI tells you how to open it. If you get a Download button, use that. If you get a block of text, paste it into Notepad on Windows. On a Mac, use TextEdit and choose Format, then Make Plain Text first. Save the file with a name ending in .html, such as bill-splitter.html, and double-click it. In Notepad, set "Save as type" to "All files", or the name may end in .txt. After each "next", replace everything in the file with the new version, save it, and reload the page.

Putting the page on the internet for other people is a bigger job, so leave it for later. To collect other people's answers, use a ready-made form tool such as Google Forms.

A skill is a ready-made set of instructions for an AI tool, so you don't have to paste the words yourself. In the Claude app, open Customize, then Skills, and turn one on. Skills need code execution switched on. On a Free, Pro or Max plan, you find it under Settings, then Capabilities. Then ask for the job in plain words, such as "Help me write a spec for a page that splits a restaurant bill".

The first skill below works in a normal chat on a paid plan. The others are made for coding tools: AI tools for programmers, which read and change a folder of files. Leave them for later. On a free plan, the six parts above cover similar ground.

That is Anthropic's advice, because a malicious skill can make Claude do things you didn't intend, or leak your data. In February 2026 the security company Snyk found 76 confirmed malicious payloads (harmful code that steals logins or data, or lets attackers back in) among nearly 4,000 skills on two marketplaces.

To learn more, try Claude Academy, Anthropic's learning site, which says its courses are free. Two suit newcomers: AI Fluency (about 4 hours) and Introduction to Claude Cowork (about 2.5 hours; no coding needed, but you need a paid Claude plan, and the desktop app for some lessons).
